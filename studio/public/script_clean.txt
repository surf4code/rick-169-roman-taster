You're born in Rome, in the year 31, and you already have an owner.

It isn't your mother. It isn't your father. It's the emperor.

Your mother is enslaved in the imperial household, which makes you one too.

Romans call a child born inside the household a verna. A house-born slave.

Which sounds polite in Latin, until you read who owns your tomorrow.

By age six, you know every back staircase on the Palatine Hill.

The imperial household—the familia Caesaris—runs like a small city under one roof.

Bakers, scribes, barbers, bath attendants, and one job nobody volunteers for.

Through a kitchen wall, you overhear an older servant whisper one word: praegustator.

Years pass as you turn nineteen. Emperor Claudius sits on the throne, and Rome is nervous.

The procurator praegustatorum—the manager of imperial tasters—calls your name.

Nobody asks what you think. You nod. You say nothing at all.

Congratulations on your promotion. Your new job title is basically human canary.

Every evening, you stand beside the imperial triclinium before the guests recline.

You learn the smells: bitter almond, sharp hemlock, acrid metallic salts hidden under honeyed wine.

You taste the water, the wine, the sauces, and every main platter in front of watching guards.

Every sip buys you five seconds of pure silence while the whole room waits to see if you sway.

You stay steady. You nod. Only then does Claudius reach for his goblet.

Three years pass, and the strangest thing happens: life gets comfortable.

The truth is, imperial tasters weren't ragged beggars—inscriptions show they had their own guild and real wealth.

You earn silver tips, wear clean wool, and start saving toward manumissio—your legal freedom.

But life in the imperial palace has other plans.

The truth is, a taster only stops a clumsy poisoner. A smart one doesn't fight the taster—they buy him.

Wait—ancient historians actually point straight at the emperor's own taster, Halotus, in AD 54.

Late at night, the senior steward corners you by the oil lamps.

He tells you quietly that if you refuse the wrong patron, you disappear before sunrise—and your mother with you.

You keep your face still. You count your breaths.

The banquet hall glows with warm bronze lamps. The platter arrives: roasted wild mushrooms.

One mushroom sits slightly apart on the silver dish. Glossy. Warm. Silent.

The room waits. Your hand hovers. You taste from the edge.

Claudius eats. Hours later, the palace doors lock from the inside.

By morning, Claudius is dead, and Nero is hailed emperor by the Praetorian Guard.

Nobody puts you on trial. Nobody asks what you tasted. You survive—and you never sleep soundly again.

In real history, Tacitus and Suetonius both record the rumor that Claudius's taster Halotus delivered the fatal dish—though modern historians still debate whether it was poison or illness.

And the ultimate Roman irony? Years later, Emperor Galba didn't execute Halotus—he promoted him to a lucrative procuratorship.

In Ancient Rome, surviving dinner wasn't the hard part. Surviving the promotion was.
